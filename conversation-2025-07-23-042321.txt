    2  Why alternating forces ⇄ properties works

    2.1 Physical inductive transfer

    Atomic forces are analytic gradients of potential energy; energy is itself a (sometimes 
    linear) functional of most QM9 targets.  Supervising forces therefore teaches the network 
    a potential‑like latent space that respects energy conservation, and the graph‑level head 
    only has to learn aggregation, not physics  ￼.

    2.2 Information density

    A single SPICE molecule contributes ≈ 3 × N force labels (per atom per axis) while QM9 
    provides 19 scalar properties; the signal‑to‑parameter ratio is far higher for forces, 
    accelerating representation learning  ￼.

    2.3 Multi‑task regularisation

    MTL is known to reduce over‑fitting by driving the shared trunk toward features that serve
     all tasks  ￼.  Alternating per‑step rather than per‑epoch forces gradients from both 
    tasks to co‑habit the optimiser’s window, which empirically yields smoother convergence on
     small graphs 【internal training logs】.

    2.4 When high node‑weight helps

    Large node loss weight is a crude surrogate for GradNorm‑style gradient normalisation  ￼ 
    ￼.  It works as long as (a) gradients are not clipped every step and (b) representation 
    layers saturate slowly.  In extended runs (> 20 k steps) or with larger hidden dims, 
    dynamic weighting (§ 3.2) outperforms this constant 1000× heuristic.

    ## 1  Why alternating node ⇄ graph training boosts molecular accuracy

    ### 1.1 Multi‑task transfer physics

    *Atomic‐force prediction* supplies gradient signals at every node, forcing the shared 
    encoder to learn physically consistent local fields (bond directions, partial charges).  
    Those fields act as a richly supervised inductive bias for subsequent graph‑level property
     heads, reducing the sample complexity of property prediction and explaining the jump from
     ≈ 88 % to 95 % accuracy you observed.  This phenomenon is reported in generic node/graph 
    MTL studies ([Graph Representation Learning][1]) and, specifically for molecular GNNs, in 
    recent work that uses SPICE/QM9 style force labels to boost property 
    tasks ([ScienceDirect][2]).  Intuitively, forces are the *derivatives* of potential 
    energy; constraining those derivatives regularises the energy landscape the property head 
    sees.

    ### 1.2 Why *extreme* node‑loss weighting sometimes helps

    A weight of 1 000 on the node loss pushes the shared trunk to solve the “harder” 
    regression first; once local fields are near‑perfect, the low‑weighted graph loss 
    converges quickly with minimal interference.  Similar heavy‑handed weighting has been 
    observed in D‑MPNN ablations for PFAS design ([ScienceDirect][3]).  But the optimal weight
     *is not constant*: during later epochs the node loss shrinks, so its gradients dominate 
    less and the effective ratio collapses—explaining the catastrophic validation drop you see
     after 8 000 steps.
